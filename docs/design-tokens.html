Tokens & component specs

Every value read out of src/theme/index.js and src/data/routineStyles.js as they stand today — not from a design file, and not from memory. Where the app and this page disagree, the app is right and this is stale.

01 — Architecture

Two layers, two collections

Primitives are the raw palette. They are named for what they are — neutral/975, orange/500 — carry no meaning, and have no modes. A primitive is the same colour in light and dark; it is simply used in one or the other.

Semantic tokens are named for the job — surface/page, text/secondary — and hold two modes, each one pointing at a primitive rather than repeating a hex. That pointer is the whole value of the split: change neutral/975 once and every dark surface built on it follows, in Figma and in code alike.

Components only ever consume semantic tokens. If a component references a primitive directly, that is the bug — it means a colour was chosen for how it looks rather than for what it does, and it will not follow a theme change.

Multiple modes on one collection is a paid Figma feature. On the free plan you get one. The workaround: build semantic/light and semantic/dark as two collections with identical variable names, both aliasing the same single primitives collection. Nothing needs renaming if you upgrade later — you merge the two into one collection with two modes.
02 — Primitives

The raw palette

Neutral

Warm, not grey — the whole ramp leans slightly yellow, which is why a neutral or blue-grey substitution reads as a different product. Twelve steps, numbered by lightness.

Each scheme uses seven of them. Five belong to light, five to dark, and two — the ink extremes — are shared, because a routine's text is black or white regardless of which scheme the app is in. The L and D marks below say which is which, so you can build one scheme's variables without wading through the other's.

The gaps at 300→500 and 600→800 are real. There are no mid-tone neutrals because nothing in the app needed one — every surface is either near-white or near-black, and the two greys that exist are both text. Do not fill the gaps to make the ramp look tidy; an unused step is a step someone will eventually reach for by accident.

This was fifteen steps until 14 August. Three pairs sat close enough that nobody could name the difference and each was two decisions to keep in step forever, so line/hairline and control/track became one neutral in both schemes, and the dark gradient's bottom stop became the raised surface. Two near-identical pairs were deliberately kept: neutral/0 with neutral/25, and neutral/975 with neutral/1000. They are the ink extremes, and collapsing them drags two already-marginal contrast pairs the wrong way — white on the danger fill from 4.42 to 4.34, black on the full-body card from 4.48 to 4.24.

Two of these are not text colours. text/primary is also the fill of the primary button, the selected segmented cell and the switch track, with text/on-ink on top. Repaint it and those surfaces move too. And text/on-ink in dark is the same primitive as surface/page — that is correct, not a mistake: a filled surface in dark mode is light, so what sits on it is the page colour.

05 — Type

Type scale

Two system faces, split by job: SF Pro Rounded for what is looked at, SF Pro for what is read and operated. Nothing is bundled. Tracking is written as a ratio of the size, so changing a size carries its tracking with it. Case is part of the role — four roles uppercase themselves and the copy stays in sentence case.

Max scale is how far each role follows the phone’s text-size setting. Prose is uncapped because it is the reason the setting exists; everything in a box the layout fixes is bounded. In Figma this is a note on the style — no visual effect at 1×, but the difference between a layout that survives accessibility sizes and one that clips.

Never set a line height below the font’s own. SF Pro’s natural line is 1.1777 em and React Native clips to the line box, so a tighter value cuts the tops and tails off — worst exactly where it shows most. Get tightness from margins instead. In Figma, leave line height on Auto for display type and control the gap with spacing.

07 — Components

Buttons, and the hierarchy

Every button is a pill, 52 minimum height, with control as its label. What separates the levels is the fill — never the size, never the radius, never the type. One filled primary per screen.

1

Primary — filled ink build first

The one committing action on a screen. On neutral screens the fill is text/primary and the label text/on-ink; on a routine screen it inverts — fill is the routine’s ink, label is the routine’s base, so the button is the screen’s colour in negative.

2

Secondary — translucent wash build first

An alternative that belongs to the screen rather than sitting on top of it. Fill is the routine’s solved wash — a translucent film over the screen colour, not an opaque panel. Same geometry as primary.

3

Tertiary — bare label or icon

No surface. Navigation, dismissal, “see all”: the close control, the back chevron, the next-exercise signpost. 44 × 44 hit area whatever the glyph measures.

4

Destructive

Only behind a swipe, and only with a confirmation after it. Fill state/danger, icon text/on-ink. Never a resting button on a screen.

The icon button is a separate component, not a small primary. 44 × 44, pill, and its surface is a blur rather than a fill — the restack control on Home is the reference. Building it as a size variant of the primary produces the wrong thing.

09

Two things not to model

  • The wash. Solved at runtime from the card, the sweep and the label’s contrast, and it differs per routine and per mode. The values here are for reference — it is not a colour to design.
  • Card tilt. The angles — 3, −5, −2, 0, 4, −3 — belong to the position in the pile, not to the routine. Reorder the stack and the sequence stays put while the routines move through it.
