Tokens & component specs

Every value read out of src/theme/index.js and src/data/routineStyles.js as they stand today — not from a design file, and not from memory. Where the app and this page disagree, the app is right and this is stale.

01 — Architecture

Three layers, three collections

Primitives are the raw palette, named for what they are — neutral/975, orange/500. No meaning, and no modes: a primitive is the same colour in light and dark, it is simply used in one or the other.

Semantic tokens are named for the job — surface/page, text/secondary — and hold two modes, each pointing at a primitive rather than repeating a hex. That pointer is the value of the split: change neutral/975 once and every dark surface built on it follows, in Figma and in code alike.

Component tokens belong to one thing and nothing else — the switch's track, the tab bar's indicator. They exist so a value used in exactly one place cannot be mistaken for a system-wide decision. There are only five, and each is named for the component that owns it.

A component may consume semantic tokens and its own component tokens. It must never reference a primitive directly: that means a colour was chosen for how it looks rather than what it does, and it will not follow a theme change.

Multiple modes on one collection is a paid Figma feature. On the free plan you get one. The workaround: build the moded collections twice — semantic/light and semantic/dark — with identical variable names, both aliasing the single primitives collection. Nothing needs renaming if you upgrade; you merge the pair into one collection with two modes.

Figma sorts alphabetically within a group. The routine list further down is in stack order, which the panel will not preserve — do not try to make it, and do not number the names to force it.
02 — Primitives

The raw palette

Neutral

Warm, not grey — the whole ramp leans slightly yellow, which is why a neutral or blue-grey substitution reads as a different product. Twelve steps, numbered by lightness.

Each scheme uses seven of them. Five belong to light, five to dark, and two — the ink extremes — are shared, because a routine's text is black or white regardless of which scheme the app is in.

The gaps at 300→500 and 600→800 are real. There are no mid-tone neutrals because nothing needed one — every surface is near-white or near-black, and the two greys that exist are both text. Do not fill them to make the ramp tidy; an unused step is one someone reaches for by mistake.

line/hairline and control/track share a neutral in both schemes: they were 3.8 and 2.7 apart, differences nobody could name and two decisions to keep in step forever. Two near-identical pairs were deliberately kept — neutral/0 with neutral/25, and neutral/975 with neutral/1000. They are the ink extremes, and collapsing them drags two already-marginal contrast pairs the wrong way.

The step numbers are role positions, not measured lightness. They agree less than they used to now that the washes are in the ramp: yellow/500 is lighter than yellow/300, lime/700 lighter than lime/600, and blue/950 is lighter than blue/900 — a routine's dark wash being lighter than its light one. That is the designer's choice showing through, not a naming error, but you cannot sort a ramp by its number and expect a gradient.

red/900 is red/700. Full body light's wash is exactly its sweep colour — the one value the old solver produced that survived the retuning — so it aliases rather than being a primitive of its own.

The 900/950 steps are only ever drawn with an opacity, given in the Role column and again on the semantic token. A wash at full strength is not a colour the app ever paints — and it is never painted directly on the routine colour either. See the note under Routine tokens: there is a blurred, tinted surface between the two.

Core tokens

Two modes each, both pointing at a primitive. Where a value carries an opacity, the alias is the primitive and the percentage is set on the layer.

text/primary is not only a text colour. It is also the fill of the primary button, the selected segmented cell and the switch when it is on, with text/on-ink on top. Repaint it and those surfaces move too.

text/on-ink in dark is the same primitive as surface/page. Correct, not a slip: a filled surface in dark mode is light, so what sits on it is the page colour.

border/default was called line/hairline. A hairline is a stroke weight, and naming a colour after one invites someone to look here for a thickness.

surface/floating is drawn by nothing. It came across from the web app, where a translucent fill and a backdrop-filter made a frosted header together. Here the blur already frosts with a tint of its own, so laying this on top of it came out very nearly solid — the blur is the whole surface, and the fill lost its job. Do not build a variable for it.

surface/card-translucent is nearly in the same position: it has exactly one consumer, the wash behind the restack button on Home, which makes it a component token wearing a semantic name. Left where it is for now because unlike the one above it is at least drawn.
03b — Component

Component tokens

Five values, each belonging to exactly one component. They were in the semantic layer, which implied a system-wide decision where there was only a local one — control/track reads as though any control might use it, and only the switch ever does.

These alias primitives rather than semantic tokens, because no semantic role fits: an unfilled switch track is not a border, even where the two share a value today. Coupling them would mean repainting one repaints the other by accident.

These belong to the tab bar, not to “glass”. They were named as though they described any frosted surface. They do not: the page titles and the restack button use the same blurred component without them. Only the tab bar draws its own edge and its own lit gradient, and only because it has to imply a material the phone will not give it until there is a development build.

The pill marking the current tab is the one to watch when repainting: light mode marks the current tab by darkening it, which on a dark bar would mark it by making it disappear. It inverts rather than shifting.

The wash is not the button. On screen the button is three layers, and a mock built from the wash alone will not match:

routine colour — or the sweep, once it has passed under — then a blurred, tinted surface, then the wash, then the label. The middle layer is missing from any drawing that treats wash as a fill, and on a flat ground it does most of the visible work: a blur has nothing to blur when what is under it is solid, but its tint still lays a film over everything.

The tint follows the routine's ink, not the scheme. A white-inked routine gets a dark glass, a black-inked one gets a light glass — the frost reinforces the ground rather than inverting it. Tinting a pale routine dark is what once made this read as a grey slab laid on top.

To approximate it in Figma: a background-blur effect on the button, plus a black fill beneath the wash for white-inked routines and a white one for black-inked routines. There is no exact opacity to give you — intensity={40} maps onto a native iOS material composited by the system on its own curve, so set it by eye against a screenshot.

The same is true of the tab bar and the page titles. Both are the same blurred surface, so any mock of those has the same missing layer.
Lower body's ink is the only one that differs between modes — black in light, white in dark. Every other routine wears the same ink in both, so it is easy to mistype as matching.

The wash is no longer solved. It used to be computed from a perceptual step, a floor against the sweep and a check on the label. All twelve are stated now, and every one came back a shade of its own routine rather than the neutral film four of them used to use.
05 — Type

Type scale

Two system faces, split by job: SF Pro Rounded for what is looked at, SF Pro for what is read and operated. Nothing is bundled. Max scale is how far each role follows the phone’s text-size setting. Prose is uncapped because it is the reason the setting exists; everything in a box the layout fixes is bounded. In Figma this is a note on the style — no visual effect at 1×, but the difference between a layout that survives accessibility sizes and one that clips.

Never set a line height below the font’s own. SF Pro’s natural line is 1.1777 em and React Native clips to the line box, so a tighter value cuts the tops and tails off — worst exactly where it shows most. Get tightness from margins instead. In Figma, leave line height on Auto for display type and control the gap with spacing.

07 — Components

Buttons, and the hierarchy

Every button is a pill, 52 minimum height, with control as its label. What separates the levels is the fill — never the size, never the radius, never the type. One filled primary per screen.

1

Primary — filled ink build first

The one committing action on a screen. On neutral screens the fill is text/primary and the label text/on-ink; on a routine screen it inverts — fill is the routine’s ink, label is the routine’s base, so the button is the screen’s colour in negative.

2

Secondary — translucent wash build first

An alternative that belongs to the screen rather than sitting on top of it. Fill is the routine’s solved wash — a translucent film over the screen colour, not an opaque panel. Same geometry as primary.

routine wash, translucent over whatever is behind

Careful

3

Tertiary — bare label or icon

No surface. Navigation, dismissal, “see all”: the close control, the back chevron, the next-exercise signpost. 44 × 44 hit area whatever the glyph measures.

4

Destructive

Only behind a swipe, and only with a confirmation after it. Fill state/danger, icon text/on-ink. Never a resting button on a screen.

The icon button is a separate component, not a small primary. 44 × 44, pill, and its surface is a blur rather than a fill — the restack control on Home is the reference. Building it as a size variant of the primary produces the wrong thing.

09

Two things not to model

  • The wash. Solved at runtime from the card, the sweep and the label’s contrast, and it differs per routine and per mode. The values here are for reference — it is not a colour to design.
  • Card tilt. The angles — 3, −5, −2, 0, 4, −3 — belong to the position in the pile, not to the routine. Reorder the stack and the sequence stays put while the routines move through it.
